The reviewers are correct: the proposed thesis contains a valid **information-loss observation**, but its central conclusion is false. The Data Processing Inequality proves only that a particular representation cannot increase Shannon mutual information under a specified Markov condition. It does **not** prove that the representation is computationally unnecessary, statistically suboptimal, or inferior for solving \(Y\).

The thesis could become strong if reformulated as a theory of the trade-off among **information, computational accessibility, numerical stability, topology, and communication rate**.

## Part I: The flawed deduction

### 1. What DPI actually proves

If
\[
X\to H\to T
\]
is a Markov chain, then
\[
I(X;T)\le I(X;H).
\]

This is an average, distribution-dependent statement about Shannon mutual information. It does not imply any of the following:

\[
I(X;Y\mid T)\le I(X;Y\mid H),
\]
\[
\Pr[\widehat Y(T)=Y]\le \Pr[\widehat Y(H)=Y],
\]
or
\[
\operatorname{Comp}(T)\le \operatorname{Comp}(H).
\]

Indeed, \(T\) may be a deliberately designed sufficient statistic for \(Y\), even when it discards large amounts of information about \(X\). The relevant condition for prediction is not preservation of \(X\), but preservation of target-relevant information:
\[
Y\perp X\mid T.
\]
Under this condition,
\[
I(X;T)\ll I(X;H)
\]
may coexist with perfect prediction from \(T\).

A simple counterexample is:
\[
X=(Y,N),\qquad H=X,\qquad T=Y,
\]
where \(N\) is independent nuisance information. Then
\[
I(X;T)=H(Y)<H(X)=I(X;H),
\]
yet \(T\) is maximally useful for predicting \(Y\), while the extra information in \(H\) is irrelevant.

There is also no general ordering between \(I(X;Y\mid T)\) and \(I(X;Y\mid H)\). Conditioning can either increase or decrease conditional mutual information because conditioning may remove confounding or induce dependence. DPI alone does not compare these quantities.

### 2. Shannon information is not computationally accessible information

Two representations can contain comparable or ordered Shannon information while differing radically in computational usefulness.

Let \(H\) encode a value \(z\) in a numerically precise vector, but let extracting the relevant predicate require solving a computationally hard problem:
\[
Y=f(H),
\]
where \(f\) is hard for a bounded observer. Let \(T\) instead contain an explicit intermediate certificate or decomposition:
\[
T=(t_1,\ldots,t_m),
\]
from which \(Y\) is recoverable by a shallow circuit.

Then it is possible that
\[
I(X;T)\le I(X;H)
\]
but
\[
\operatorname{Time}_{\mathcal A}(Y\mid T)
\ll
\operatorname{Time}_{\mathcal A}(Y\mid H)
\]
for a computationally bounded algorithm \(\mathcal A\).

A useful formal distinction is:

- **Shannon information:** \(I(X;Z)\).
- **Computational information:** the success of a restricted algorithm class \(\mathcal C\) in predicting \(Y\) from \(Z\).
- **Descriptional information:** Kolmogorov complexity \(K(Z)\).
- **Communication information:** the number of bits or symbols transmitted.
- **Numerical information:** precision and conditioning under perturbation.

For a hypothesis class \(\mathcal C\), define
\[
\operatorname{Adv}_{\mathcal C}(Z;Y)
=
\sup_{g\in\mathcal C}
\left|
\Pr[g(Z)=Y]-\max_y\Pr[Y=y]
\right|.
\]
There is no general monotonicity theorem saying that
\[
I(X;Z_1)\le I(X;Z_2)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Adv}_{\mathcal C}(Z_1;Y)
\le
\operatorname{Adv}_{\mathcal C}(Z_2;Y).
\]

The missing object in the proposed thesis is therefore a **resource-bounded data-processing inequality**, perhaps of the form
\[
\operatorname{Adv}_{\mathcal C}(T;Y)
\le
\operatorname{Adv}_{\mathcal C'}(H;Y)
+\varepsilon,
\]
where \(\mathcal C'\) accounts for the extra computational resources required to decode \(H\). Without such a theorem, DPI says nothing about bounded computation.

### 3. The Markov-chain assumption is often false for scratchpads

The claimed chain
\[
X\to H\to T\to Y
\]
requires that \(Y\) depend on \(X,H\) only through \(T\). In an autoregressive system, however, later hidden states may depend on both the original input and the generated tokens:
\[
H_{t+1}
=
F_\theta(H_t,X,T_{\le t}),
\qquad
T_{t+1}\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid H_t,T_{\le t},X).
\]

Thus a more realistic process is
\[
X\to H_0\to T_1\to H_1\to T_2\to\cdots\to Y,
\]
with skip connections from \(X\) and persistent continuous state. The generated text is not necessarily a one-way lossy channel through which all future computation must pass.

Even if the strict chain holds, DPI applies to the representation map, not to the total algorithmic system. A scratchpad may lose information per symbol while adding computational steps, memory, error correction, and modular structure.

### 4. Autoregressive tokens as external computational time

A fixed-depth transformer has a bounded computational graph. Depending on the precise model—uniformity, precision, positional encoding, fan-in, attention restrictions, and allowed arithmetic—its expressivity may be related to circuit classes such as \(\mathrm{AC}^0\), \(\mathrm{TC}^0\), or other nonuniform classes. These classifications are highly model-dependent and should not be stated as a universal “transformers are TC\(^0\)” theorem.

Autoregressive execution changes the model. At step \(t\), the generated token becomes writable state that is reread at later steps:
\[
s_{t+1}=F_\theta(s_t,x_t),
\qquad
x_{t+1}=G_\theta(s_{t+1}).
\]

With an unbounded or polynomially growing sequence, this creates a time-indexed external memory. The total computation is a composition
\[
F^{(T)}
=
F_T\circ F_{T-1}\circ\cdots\circ F_1,
\]
whose effective depth is \(T\) times the depth of one invocation.

This can yield substantially more sequential computation, but several qualifications are essential:

- The sequence length must be allowed to grow with the input size.
- The model must be able to write and reliably reread state.
- Positional encodings must support the required addressing.
- The token alphabet and precision must provide sufficient memory.
- The computation must be uniform and efficiently generated.
- “Turing completeness” requires a formal computational model and usually unbounded time or precision.

A finite-context transformer with fixed precision and bounded output length is not thereby Turing complete.

The correct claim is conditional:

> Autoregressive execution can simulate a sequential machine when generated symbols function as writable, addressable, persistent state.

It does not follow that the symbols preserve more Shannon information than the continuous state.

### 5. Discretization as error correction

Continuous recurrence can accumulate perturbations. Consider
\[
h_{t+1}=F(h_t)+\eta_t.
\]
If \(F\) has Lipschitz constant \(L\), then
\[
\|h_t-\widetilde h_t\|
\le
L^t\|h_0-\widetilde h_0\|
+
\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}L^{t-1-j}\|\eta_j\|.
\]

For \(L>1\), perturbations can grow exponentially; for \(L=1\), they can grow linearly. A quantizer with attractor regions can instead implement
\[
Q(h)=a_i
\quad\text{for }h\in B_i,
\]
where each basin \(B_i\) maps to a stable symbolic state \(a_i\). If perturbations remain inside the basin, the decoded state is unchanged:
\[
\|h-\widetilde h\|<r_i
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Q(h)=Q(\widetilde h).
\]

This is a genuine robustness advantage, but it is not free. Quantization introduces:

- approximation error,
- finite rate,
- boundary sensitivity,
- possible irreversible information loss,
- synchronization and parsing costs.

The right comparison is not “continuous bad, discrete good,” but the stability of two channels:
\[
\text{continuous channel: }h_{t+1}=F(h_t)+\eta_t,
\]
\[
\text{discrete channel: }s_{t+1}=Q(F(s_t)+\eta_t).
\]

Discrete attractors act like error-correcting codes only if basin margins, transition margins, and decoding error probabilities are formally established.

## Part II: What a rigorous thesis would need

### 1. Rate-distortion formulation

Let \(Z\in\mathcal M\) be a continuous latent variable and \(T\in\Sigma^*\) a variable-length token sequence. Let \(d:\mathcal M\times\mathcal M\to\mathbb R_+\) be a distortion measure, and let \(R\) be the expected number of transmitted bits.

The rate-distortion function is
\[
R(D)
=
\inf_{p(\hat Z\mid Z):\mathbb E[d(Z,\hat Z)]\le D}
I(Z;\hat Z).
\]

A finite-token representation with alphabet size \(|\Sigma|\) and expected length \(L\) has rate at most
\[
R\le L\log_2|\Sigma|.
\]

Therefore, if
\[
L\log_2|\Sigma|<R(D),
\]
then distortion below \(D\) is impossible for any encoder-decoder pair under the chosen source distribution and distortion metric.

This is the precise form of a bottleneck theorem. It must be target-relative: distortion of the full latent \(Z\) may be irrelevant if only a task statistic \(S(Z)\) is needed.

### 2. Metric entropy of the sphere

For the unit sphere \(S^{D-1}\subset\mathbb R^D\), under Euclidean distance, the covering number satisfies, up to universal constants,
\[
\left(\frac{c}{\varepsilon}\right)^{D-1}
\lesssim
N(S^{D-1},\varepsilon)
\lesssim
\left(\frac{C}{\varepsilon}\right)^{D-1}.
\]

Hence its \(\varepsilon\)-capacity is
\[
\log_2 N(S^{D-1},\varepsilon)
=
\Theta\!\left((D-1)\log_2\frac1\varepsilon\right).
\]

A token stream of length \(L\) over alphabet \(\Sigma\) has at most
\[
|\Sigma|^L
\]
distinct messages and therefore at most
\[
L\log_2|\Sigma|
\]
bits of combinatorial capacity.

Consequently, uniform \(\varepsilon\)-approximation of arbitrary points on \(S^{D-1}\) requires
\[
L
=
\Omega\!\left(
\frac{(D-1)\log(1/\varepsilon)}
{\log|\Sigma|}
\right).
\]

For \(D=4096\), this is a large rate requirement when high precision is demanded. But it does not prove that tokenization is inferior for a task. It proves only that arbitrary geometric reconstruction is expensive.

### 3. Stiefel and Grassmann manifolds

The real Stiefel manifold
\[
\mathrm{St}(D,k)
=
\{U\in\mathbb R^{D\times k}:U^\top U=I_k\}
\]
has dimension
\[
\dim \mathrm{St}(D,k)
=
Dk-\frac{k(k+1)}2.
\]

The Grassmann manifold
\[
\mathrm{Gr}(k,D)
\]
has dimension
\[
\dim \mathrm{Gr}(k,D)=k(D-k).
\]

Under standard Riemannian metrics, their small-scale covering numbers obey
\[
\log N(\mathcal M,\varepsilon)
=
\dim(\mathcal M)\log\frac1\varepsilon+O_{\mathcal M}(1).
\]

Thus arbitrary \(\varepsilon\)-accurate coding requires at least
\[
L
=
\Omega\!\left(
\frac{\dim(\mathcal M)\log(1/\varepsilon)}
{\log|\Sigma|}
\right)
\]
tokens.

This establishes a finite-rate lower bound. It does **not** establish an impossibility of using one-dimensional sequences: a sequence can encode high-dimensional objects, provided it is sufficiently long. “One-dimensional” refers to organization, not necessarily information dimension.

### 4. Why Bourgain does not prove the claimed obstruction

Bourgain’s theorem states that every \(n\)-point metric space admits an embedding into Euclidean space with distortion \(O(\log n)\)[1][2]. It is a theorem about finite metric spaces and a target norm, not a theorem that high-dimensional manifolds cannot be represented by sequences.

Several distinctions matter:

- A token sequence is a discrete code, not automatically a one-dimensional metric embedding.
- An arbitrary sequence can encode a vector losslessly if its length is sufficient.
- Bourgain gives an upper bound for embedding finite metrics; it does not establish a universal lower bound for \(\mathrm{Gr}(k,D)\) into strings.
- “Topological tearing” applies to continuous injective maps under particular dimension and regularity assumptions, not to discontinuous digital encoders.
- A discrete code need not preserve geodesic distances, local neighborhoods, or topology at all.

A legitimate obstruction theorem would need to specify:

1. the source metric;
2. the target metric on \(\Sigma^*\);
3. fixed or variable sequence length;
4. continuity, Lipschitzness, or computational restrictions;
5. required distortion;
6. whether decoding must be deterministic, local, online, or efficient.

Without these specifications, the topological argument is invalid.

### 5. Continuous attractor memory

A continuous memory system can be modeled on a manifold \(\mathcal M\) by
\[
\dot h(t)
=
-\operatorname{grad}_{\mathcal M}E(h(t);X),
\]
or discretely by
\[
h_{t+1}
=
\operatorname{Exp}_{h_t}
\left(
-\eta_t\operatorname{grad}_{\mathcal M}E(h_t;X)
\right).
\]

If \(E\) has attractors \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\), then a basin
\[
B_i=\{h_0:\lim_{t\to\infty}h(t)=a_i\}
\]
stores a discrete state continuously. Robustness follows if perturbations remain within basin margins.

A modern Hopfield-style update can be written schematically as
\[
h^+
=
\operatorname{softmax}(\beta K^\top h)^\top K,
\]
where \(K\) stores patterns and \(\beta\) is an inverse-temperature parameter. Under suitable separation and scaling assumptions, the dynamics can retrieve stored patterns as attractors.

This supplies a compelling research direction: compare symbolic and continuous memory under common constraints:

\[
\text{memory budget},\quad
\text{noise variance},\quad
\text{retrieval error},\quad
\text{update cost},\quad
\text{number of sequential steps}.
\]

But continuous attractors do not automatically provide unbounded computational depth. A recurrent system gains depth only through repeated updates, and then it faces its own stability, convergence, controllability, and precision requirements.

### 6. A possible computational model

Define two architectures.

**Token architecture**
\[
s_{t+1}=F_\theta(s_t,x_t),\qquad
t_t\in\Sigma,\qquad
x_{t+1}=G_\theta(s_{t+1},t_{\leq t}).
\]

**Continuous architecture**
\[
h_{t+1}
=
\Phi_\theta(h_t,x_t)+\eta_t,
\qquad
h_t\in\mathcal M.
\]

For a task distribution \(\mathcal D\), define:

- sample complexity \(n_{\mathrm{tok}}(\epsilon,\delta)\);
- \(n_{\mathrm{cont}}(\epsilon,\delta)\);
- expected latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{tok}},\tau_{\mathrm{cont}}\);
- bit error \(p_{\mathrm{tok}},p_{\mathrm{cont}}\);
- computational cost \(C_{\mathrm{tok}},C_{\mathrm{cont}}\);
- memory rate \(R_{\mathrm{tok}},R_{\mathrm{cont}}\).

A theorem that continuous streaming strictly dominates tokenization would need assumptions implying
\[
n_{\mathrm{cont}}(\epsilon,\delta)
<
n_{\mathrm{tok}}(\epsilon,\delta),
\]
\[
p_{\mathrm{cont}}<p_{\mathrm{tok}},
\]
\[
\tau_{\mathrm{cont}}<\tau_{\mathrm{tok}},
\]
and perhaps
\[
C_{\mathrm{cont}}\le C_{\mathrm{tok}}.
\]

Those inequalities cannot hold universally. There are immediate counterexamples:

- If \(Y\) is a symbolic function of a short certificate, tokens can be statistically and computationally optimal.
- If the channel is noisy and the continuous state has no error-correcting margin, symbolic attractors can have lower error.
- If arbitrary high-precision manifold reconstruction is required, continuous transmission can be more rate-efficient.
- If long sequential computation is required, autoregressive external memory may outperform a fixed-depth continuous map.
- If the continuous system uses repeated recurrence, its latency may exceed token generation.
